# Test Yourself, Sleep Well, Skip the Pills

The most effective, best-evidenced ways to retain what you learn are not products. They are two study behaviors and one physiological habit. **Retrieval practice** (recalling information from memory instead of rereading it) and **spaced practice** (spreading that recall over days and weeks) are the only techniques that the anchor review rated "high utility," and pooled effects for combining them reach about **g = 0.74** ([Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)†). **Sleep** is the only lifestyle factor with a medium-sized effect: losing a night before learning impairs encoding by g ≈ 0.62, or about 0.46 after correcting for publication bias ([Newbury et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8893218/); [Rastle Lab](https://www.rastlelab.com/post/sleep-deprivation-impairs-learning-and-memory-but-now-is-the-time-for-replication-science)†). For a mid-career DoD financial systems professional who must hold FMR rules, appropriations logic, SQL and DAX patterns, and a second business in their head at once, this has a practical meaning. About 15–20 minutes a day of spaced self-testing, problem-style practice on real queries and measures, AI used as a quizzer rather than an answer machine, and protected sleep around heavy learning will do more than any supplement stack, brain-training app or "second brain" setup. Supplements, smart drugs, brain games and consumer brain stimulation produce small effects at best (about 0.1–0.3 SD, mostly in older or deficient people). Several were the subject of federal enforcement for unsupported claims. The weak link in the evidence is that almost no study enrolls healthy 30–50-year-old professionals learning technical material on the job. The plan below extrapolates from student, lab and older-adult populations, and says where it does.

*How to read the figures: a dagger (†) marks a number taken only from an abstract, press release, repository record or secondary summary, because the full text was blocked or not retrieved during research. Unmarked figures were read from full text (PMC, author PDFs, or primary data). Verify † figures against the original paper before quoting them in anything formal.*

## Strong evidence clusters around practices; hype clusters around products

The literature divides cleanly. Every intervention with replicated, meta-analytic support is something you *do* while learning or sleeping. Most heavily marketed interventions are something you *buy*. The scorecard below grades each option on a common scale. **Strong** means several meta-analyses agree on a meaningful effect. **Moderate** means one meta-analysis or a conditional effect. **Weak** means small, inconsistent or subgroup-only effects. **Null/Hype** means well-tested failure or enforcement action.

Two cautions apply across the whole table. First, the publication bias seen in the sleep literature probably affects most of these fields, so bias-adjusted or replication numbers are the safer ones to plan around. Second, the Donoghue and Hattie meta-analysis of 242 studies found that most outcomes measured were **surface or factual learning**, and effects were larger for lower-ability learners ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/text)†). An experienced analyst learning relational material such as how FMR volumes interact with appropriation law should expect real but smaller gains than the headline numbers.

## Retrieval and spacing beat rereading, highlighting and handwriting

Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten popular study techniques. They rated practice testing and distributed practice "high utility" across ages, abilities and subjects. They rated rereading, highlighting, summarization, the keyword mnemonic and imagery for text "low utility" ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)†). Low utility does not mean zero. Underlining and summarization still showed "relatively high effects" against weak comparison conditions ([Donoghue & Hattie 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/text)†). They spend time that self-testing would use better. Bjork's account of desirable difficulties explains why the weaker habits persist. Fluent rereading raises *retrieval strength*, which is how accessible something feels right now. It does little for *storage strength*, which slows forgetting. The lower an item's current accessibility when you successfully recall it, the bigger the long-term gain ([Bjork & Bjork 2011](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)). Effective study should therefore feel harder and produce lower practice scores. Judge progress by delayed tests, not by how smooth a session felt.

The format of the test matters. Rowland's meta-analysis found that **recall-type tests outperform recognition tests** ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/)†). Writing a query or stating a rule from a blank prompt beats picking the right answer from a list. Testing also transfers beyond the exact item practised. Pan and Rickard's analysis of 192 effect sizes found an overall transfer effect of **d ≈ 0.40**. Transfer was strongest to new question formats and to application and inference questions, and weakest to simply rearranged item pairs ([Pan & Rickard 2018](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018.pdf)). That favors scenario questions ("a reimbursable order arrives with this fund citation — what applies?") over verbatim recall of regulation text. Feedback after a failed retrieval is a sensible default, although Adesope found mixed evidence that feedback adds to the testing effect ([WSU PDF](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)).

Spacing has a usable rule of thumb. In a study of more than 1,350 people tested up to a year later, the optimal gap between sessions was about **20–40% of a one-week retention horizon** and about **5–10% of a one-year horizon**. Gaps that are too long cost far less than gaps that are too short ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0kp5q19x/qt0kp5q19x.pdf?t=lnry4n)). Expanding schedules were not reliably better than evenly spaced ones (g = 0.034, ns) ([Latimier 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)†), so the number of successful spaced retrievals matters more than the exact pattern. These ratios come from essentially one research group and mostly verbal material. Treat the table below as dosing guidance, not a law.

| You need to remember it for | First review gap (approx.) | Workplace example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 week | 1–2 days | Briefing to leadership next Friday |
| 1 month | ~1 week | Mid-year review execution numbers |
| 3 months | ~2–3 weeks | A certification exam next quarter |
| 1 year or more | ~3–5 weeks, then ongoing | FMR structure, DAX time-intelligence patterns, appropriation rules |

Two popular beliefs fail scrutiny. Matching instruction to a preferred "learning style" has **no adequate evidence base**. The few properly designed tests "flatly contradict" it ([Pashler et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162104/)†). The famous finding that longhand notes beat laptop notes (n = 67) **did not replicate**. A 145-person replication and an eight-study mini-meta-analysis found small, non-significant differences ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/no-benefit-handwriting-notes.html)†), and a second direct replication called any verdict "premature" ([Morehead et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007%2Fs10648-019-09468-2.pdf)). The robust finding is that verbatim transcription predicts worse scores, whatever the medium ([APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/no-benefit-handwriting-notes.html)†). Paraphrase, then quiz yourself from the notes rather than rereading them.

Memory palaces work, but only for a narrow kind of material. In Dresler's study, ordinary adults trained the method of loci for about 30 minutes a day over 6 weeks. Their word-list recall roughly doubled, and the gains persisted at 4 months ([Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2017/03/memorization-tool-bulks-up-brains-internal-connections.html)†). Secondary sources disagree on the exact numbers, and groups had only 17–23 people. The technique fits arbitrary ordered material such as a briefing outline or a fixed sequence of steps. It does nothing for understanding why a WHERE clause differs from HAVING. For most professionals, the roughly 20 hours of training will not pay off.

## Turning FMR rules, SQL joins and DAX measures into retained skill

No study tests spaced repetition on SQL, DAX or government financial regulations, so everything in this section is extrapolated from general learning science. The extrapolation is fairly direct, because the research separates material into types that map onto this job.

**Discrete facts** include acronyms (PPBE, USSGL, FIAR, ADA), thresholds, deadlines and function syntax. They belong in a spaced-repetition app. FSRS is the open-source scheduler now built into Anki and RemNote. On a benchmark of roughly 10,000 users' review logs, it predicts forgetting better than older algorithms (FSRS-6 log loss 0.346 vs. 0.469 for Duolingo's half-life regression) ([srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)). The widely repeated claim of **20–30% fewer reviews than Anki's legacy SM-2** comes from simulations and secondary sources, and some users report the opposite at matched settings ([Diane guide](https://www.diane.app/en/guides/fsrs-vs-sm2)†; [AnkiWeb forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/has-anyone-done-a-live-comparison-of-fsrs-and-sm2-as-implemented-in-anki-it-looks-like-no-so-can-anyone-help-me-set-it-up/34996)). Turn FSRS on with desired retention around 0.85–0.90 and stop optimizing the scheduler. Card quality matters more. Wozniak's rules say to understand the material before memorizing it and to keep each card to one minimal fact. They also recommend cloze deletions as the fastest way to turn prose such as regulation text into reviewable items ([SuperMemo](https://www.supermemo.com/en/archives1990-2015/articles/20rules)). Matuschak adds that prompts should be focused, effortful and free of yes/no questions. He also warns that people rarely notice when their own prompts are bad ([Matuschak](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/)). Never put ten acronyms on one card. Make each acronym a two-way card, plus a cloze sentence showing it in use.

**Regulations** such as the DoD FMR call for memorizing the *map*, not the text. Card which volume and chapter governs a topic, the key decision rules and thresholds, and the definitions you cite most. Write each rule in your own words before you card it, and use "where would you look to determine X?" prompts. Keep the authoritative text in a searchable store, and revisit affected cards when chapters change. This follows the offloading research. Saving material to a *reliable* external store frees memory for new learning by reducing interference, but the benefit disappears when the store is untrustworthy ([Storm & Stone 2015](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/saving-old-information-can-boost-memory-for-new-information.html)†). Whatever you offload, you tend not to remember yourself ([Risko & Gilbert 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27542527/)†). The working rule is simple. **Offload what you need to find. Internalize what you need to think with.**

**Procedural skills** such as SQL, DAX and Excel benefit most from production and from interleaving. Practice questions outperformed flashcards per item in the best-known study of professional spaced repetition: about 445 board-style questions and about 1,700 Anki cards were each associated with one extra USMLE Step 1 point. That medical-student evidence is correlational ([Deng et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4673073)). Interleaving helps most when categories are **similar and easily confused** and when the material is complex ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)†). That describes LEFT vs. INNER JOIN, WHERE vs. HAVING, window functions vs. GROUP BY, and CALCULATE filter context vs. row context in DAX. Practice them in mixed sets, not one topic at a time. Write cards as small problems, for example "Write the DAX for fiscal-YTD obligations where the fiscal year ends 30 September," not as definitions. Explain each worked example to yourself as you study it, since prompted self-explanation outperformed explanations given by an instructor (g = 0.55) ([BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies)†).

**Generative AI** cuts both ways, and the direction depends on how you set it up. In a randomized trial with about 1,000 students, unrestricted ChatGPT-style access raised practice scores. When access was removed, those students scored **17% lower** on exams than controls. A version with tutoring safeguards largely prevented the harm ([Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/)†). In a Harvard crossover RCT, an AI tutor prompted to reveal one step at a time and never the full solution produced more learning than in-class active learning ([Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)). That study measured short-term learning only. The much-shared MIT "cognitive debt" EEG study is a small, non-peer-reviewed preprint, and it has drawn a formal methodological critique ([arXiv 2601.00856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856v1)). It should not drive decisions. The practical design follows from the two RCTs. Write the query, measure or FMR interpretation yourself first, then ask AI to critique it. Use a standing prompt such as "Ask me one question at a time about this chapter; give hints only after I attempt; list what I missed at the end." Let AI draft candidate flashcards, but edit them yourself. Keep regular no-AI repetitions, because performance with AI overstates what you have learned. In audit-sensitive DoD work, checking AI output against the FMR and OMB circulars is both a professional duty and a learning step. A Microsoft survey of 319 knowledge workers found that critical effort shifts toward verifying AI output ([Lee et al. 2025](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)).

**Teaching** links the day job to the business. Meta-analyses support learning gains from teaching others, and studying with the expectation of teaching beats studying for a test. Some studies found null results for non-interactive formats such as recorded explanations ([Educational Psychology Review 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09643-4)). A weekly five-minute recorded walkthrough or a short write-up consolidates the material and also produces reusable content. Examples include an internal how-to on a Power BI execution dashboard, or a client-facing explainer on the automation product. Second-brain systems such as Zettelkasten, PARA, Obsidian or OneNote have **no controlled evidence** that they improve retention ([brennan.day critique](https://brennan.day/what-have-note-taking-pkms-accomplished-really/)). Their value lies in writing notes in your own words and pairing them with retrieval, not in the storage. PARA is still a reasonable filing system for someone separating a federal job from a business, as long as you treat it as a filing system and not a learning method.

## Sleep dominates the lifestyle levers; the rest are modest or long-term

Sleep is the one lifestyle factor whose effect on memory is comparable to good study technique. Sleep loss before learning harms encoding more (g = 0.62) than sleep loss after learning harms consolidation (g = 0.28). Both shrink after bias correction, to about 0.46 and 0.17 ([Newbury et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8893218/); [Rastle Lab](https://www.rastlelab.com/post/sleep-deprivation-impairs-learning-and-memory-but-now-is-the-time-for-replication-science)†). The more relevant finding for a budget analyst is about chronic short nights. Restricting sleep to about 3–6.5 hours produced a small impairment (g = 0.29) that was **not statistically different from total deprivation** overall ([Crowley et al. 2024](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/107573)†). Habitual 5–6-hour nights during budget submissions or end-of-year execution therefore probably cost memory formation in a way that feels normal but is not. The estimate is uncertain. Protect at least 7 hours on the nights before and after any heavy learning, such as a new FMR change, a new data model or a certification block. A 20–30-minute early-afternoon nap is a reasonable supplement. In one experiment, only the 30-minute nap improved later encoding, and that study used young adults ([NUS Medicine](https://medicine.nus.edu.sg/?p=45025)†).

Caffeine matters mostly through sleep. 400 mg taken even six hours before bed cut objectively measured sleep by more than an hour, and participants did not notice ([Drake et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3805807)). This was a 12-person study. One study found that caffeine taken after studying improved 24-hour consolidation ([Borota 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24413697/)†), but direct replications reportedly found no effect ([Tufts dissertation](https://dl.tufts.edu/concern/pdfs/bn999m46q)). Use caffeine for alertness while learning, and stop at least six hours before bed. Alcohol shows dose-dependent associations with smaller brain volume starting at about **1–2 units a day** in 36,678 UK Biobank adults. The data are observational ([Daviet et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8897479)).

Exercise helps memory most through *when* you do it, not how much you do. A bout before learning had a negligible, non-significant effect (d = 0.11). Effects were larger when exercise fell in the consolidation window after learning ([Loprinzi et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6523402)). A 10–30-minute brisk walk or ride right after a study block is therefore a cheap way to help it stick. Chronic exercise has only a small effect on near-term memory (SMD ≈ 0.15†), and secondary sources disagree on the subgroup figures ([Roig 2013 record](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/the-effects-of-cardiovascular-exercise-on-human-memory-a-review-w)). The strongest midlife reason to exercise is long-term vascular and dementia risk, not next month's recall. Claims that BDNF explains the benefit rest largely on animal data ([PMC4942640](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4942640/)).

Stress matters at the moment of recall more than over the long term. In 113 studies, acute stress shortly before or during retrieval reliably impaired recall. The size of the cortisol response did not predict how much memory was impaired ([Shields et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5436944)). For high-stakes recall, such as fielding a three-star's questions on obligation rates, prepare by retrieval-practicing the likely questions. Avoid stacking stressors in the 15–25 minutes beforehand. Mindfulness gives small cognitive effects in healthy adults (g ≈ 0.15–0.23), with larger benefits in stressed or clinical groups ([Whitfield et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34350544/)†). It is worth doing for wellbeing, not as a memory tool.

Several popular claims fail. The 604-person MIND diet RCT was **null** against a calorie-restricted control diet after 3 years ([Barnes et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10513737/)). Dehydration impairs cognition only beyond about 2% body-mass loss (ES −0.21) ([Georgia Tech](https://news.gatech.edu/news/2018/07/16/we-get-parched-cognition-can-sputter-dehydration-study-says)†). Drinking extra water does not enhance memory. The claim that a phone's mere presence drains your brain failed a preregistered replication, and one 33-study meta-analysis found d = −0.02 ([Hartanto et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10525686/)). Active use and notifications during study remain a plausible cost, but the notes did not quantify them. For the longer horizon, the 2024 Lancet Commission lists 14 modifiable dementia risk factors and estimates that about 45% of cases could be prevented or delayed. That estimate assumes causality ([Alzheimer Europe](https://www.alzheimer-europe.org/news/2024-lancet-commission-underscores-potential-dementia-risk-reduction-identifying-14-modifiable)†). Hypertension, LDL cholesterol and hearing loss are the midlife items. Intensive blood-pressure control cut mild cognitive impairment by 19% in SPRINT MIND ([NIH](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/does-intensive-blood-pressure-control-reduce-dementia)†). The ACHIEVE hearing trial was null overall and positive only in a high-risk subgroup ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478886/)†). These matter for 2045, not for next quarter's recall.

## Supplements, smart drugs and brain games fail the healthy midlife test

No supplement has strong evidence of meaningfully improving memory in healthy 30–50-year-olds, and no large RCT has enrolled that age group. The best signals are small and appear mostly in older adults or people with a deficiency. The COSMOS multivitamin trials improved episodic memory in older adults. The authors estimated the COSMOS-Web gain as equal to about 3.1 years of age-related memory change ([Yeung et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37244291/)†). Physicians' Health Study II found no cognitive benefit, and the Alzheimer's Association declined to recommend widespread use ([PMC10375458](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10375458/)). Creatine's memory effect (SMD 0.29 in healthy people) was **concentrated in adults aged 66–76 and essentially absent in younger participants** ([Prokopidis et al. 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9999677/)). Bacopa improved free recall in 9 of 17 tests, but only after 12 weeks or more, and with publication-bias concerns ([PubMed 22747190](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22747190/)†). B vitamins showed a very small episodic-memory effect (SMD −0.09, where negative favors treatment), and vitamin D showed none ([PMC7231132](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7231132/)). Ginkgo failed in the 3,069-person GEM trial ([Snitz et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2832285)). Omega-3 showed no benefit in cognitively healthy people ([Cochrane](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD005379.pub3//ta)†). Lion's Mane has only pilot trials of about 18–40 people ([PMC10675414](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10675414/)). Caffeine plus L-theanine improves alertness and attention for 1–2 hours, not memory ([Camfield 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24946991/)†). Effects of 0.1–0.3 SD sit at the edge of what a person would notice day to day. The rational move for a midlife adult is to **ask a clinician to test for B12 or vitamin D deficiency and correct any deficit**, not to supplement across the board.

Prevagen shows the marketing pattern to watch for. The company's own 218-person trial found no significant difference from placebo on any of nine tests. The FTC complaint says the data were then analysed "more than 30 different ways" to find a positive subgroup ([ConsumerLab](https://www.consumerlab.com/answers/does-prevagen-really-improve-memory/prevagen_memory/)†). In 2024 a jury found the "clinically shown" memory claims deceptive, and the court enjoined them ([NY AG](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2024/attorney-general-james-wins-trial-against-quincy-bioscience-deceptive-and); [FTC order](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/quincy_order_and_judgment.pdf)).

Prescription "smart drugs" give tiny returns for real risk. In rested healthy adults, modafinil's overall cognitive effect was g = 0.10 ([Kredlow et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31433334/)†). The authors of a 48-study meta-analysis judged stimulant memory effects "probably modest" after publication bias, and suggested users may mostly feel more motivated rather than becoming more capable ([Ilieva et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25591060/)†). One study found that healthy adults on dextroamphetamine had disturbed sleep and worse working memory the next day ([Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/326023.php)†). The net effect on memory may be negative, because the drug undermines the sleep that consolidates learning. A review of student use lists tolerance, dependence, and cardiovascular and neurological complications ([Brain Sci 2021](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/8953/1/brainsci_11_00355_v2.pdf)). For someone working in a DoD environment, there is a further cost: these are controlled substances, and using them without a prescription is illegal.

Brain-training apps make you better at the app. The FTC settled with Lumosity in 2016 for **$2 million**, with a $50 million judgment suspended, over claims about work performance and dementia protection ([FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges)). A major review found extensive gains on the trained tasks, less on closely related tasks, and "inadequate" evidence of real-world benefit ([APS / Simons et al. 2016](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/brain-training.html)). Against active controls, working-memory training showed no convincing far transfer ([Melby-Lervåg et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4968033)). The one notable exception is narrow. In the 20-year ACTIVE follow-up, older adults who did speed-of-processing training plus boosters had **25% lower claims-based dementia incidence**. The memory-training arm showed nothing, and the training software is now sold commercially ([Johns Hopkins](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/news/newsroom/news-releases/2026/02/cognitive-speed-training-linked-to-lower-dementia-incidence-up-to-20-years-later)†; [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1115860)†). That result concerns dementia risk in people over 65, not memory enhancement in midlife. Consumer tDCS headsets fare no better. The most-cited meta-analysis found no reliable single-session effect on memory in healthy adults ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/trouble-tdcs-electrical-brain-stimulation-may-not-work-after-all)†), and later analyses find at most faster but not more accurate responses ([PMC8018377](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8018377)).

## A prioritized 90-day plan that fits a day job and a business

The plan ranks actions by expected gain per hour, given about 20–30 spare minutes on most weekdays. The top three tiers cost little money. Everything listed under "skip" costs money or carries risk for little or no expected gain.

| Priority | Action | Evidence | Time cost | Concrete setup |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Daily spaced retrieval in Anki (FSRS on, retention 0.85–0.90), with one deck each for DoD finance, data/BI, and the business | Strong | 10–20 min/day | Start with the 50 acronyms and FMR "map" cards you look up most; add about 5 new cards a day; recall-style prompts only |
| 2 | Protect sleep of 7+ hours, especially the nights before and after heavy learning; caffeine cutoff 6+ hours before bed | Strong | ~0 (scheduling) | Put learning blocks before rested nights, not in the middle of a budget crunch |
| 3 | Problem-style practice for SQL, DAX and Excel: attempt first, then check | Strong (retrieval + generation), extrapolated | 2–3 × 30 min/week | Keep a "problem bank" of real anonymized tasks; mix similar types (JOIN types, filter vs. row context) in each set |
| 4 | AI in quiz or critique mode only, plus regular no-AI repetitions | Moderate (two RCTs) | Replaces passive reading | Save a standing tutor prompt; never paste a task and accept the answer while learning |
| 5 | Self-explain worked examples; one weekly teach-back (5-minute recording or short write-up) | Moderate | 30 min/week | Alternate topics between day-job and business content; reuse business explainers as marketing |
| 6 | Trusted external store for reference material; offload to find, internalize to think | Moderate (offloading lab studies); PARA itself untested | 1 hour setup, then minutes | Keep FMR links, report specs and client details in one searchable place; card only what you reason with |
| 7 | 10–30 minutes of brisk exercise right after a study block; 150 min/week overall | Moderate (timing) / Weak (near-term memory) | Fits existing routine | Walk after an evening study session |
| 8 | Before high-stakes recall, practise the likely questions and avoid stacking stressors | Strong (stress at retrieval) | 15 min | Rehearse briefing Q&A as retrieval practice the day before |
| 9 | Midlife health checks: blood pressure, LDL, hearing; test B12 and vitamin D before supplementing | Moderate (long-term dementia risk) | Annual physical | Ask the clinician for these specifically |
| Skip | Memory supplements, nootropic stacks, modafinil or stimulants, brain-training subscriptions, tDCS headsets, rereading or highlighting as review, learning-style quizzes | Null, weak or hype | — | Redirect the money and time to priorities 1–3 |

The first month should build habits, not volume. Set up the decks, write about 150 high-quality cards from material you use every week, and keep reviews under 20 minutes. Accept that practice will feel harder than rereading did. In the second month, add the SQL/DAX problem bank and the weekly teach-back, and start using AI in tutor mode. In the third month, check Anki's lapse statistics to see which cards you keep forgetting. Run a delayed self-test on material from month one, without notes or AI. The result tells you whether the system is working better than any feeling of fluency will. Cards that fail repeatedly are usually badly written, so rewrite or split them rather than grinding through them. Method-of-loci training is worth its roughly 20-hour setup only if you face a lot of arbitrary ordered material to memorize verbatim. For this role, that is rare.

## Conclusion

For this professional, the binding constraint on memory is probably not ability. It is that most time "studying" goes to recognition activities: rereading the FMR, skimming a DAX tutorial, or letting an AI write the measure. Those activities build familiarity without durable storage. The research suggests the biggest available gain is a switch in *format*, from consuming to producing, rather than any new input such as a pill, an app or a framework. Generative AI makes this distinction more urgent. Used as a crutch, it widens the gap between how competent you feel and what you can do unaided, and the best RCT measured that gap at 17% on exams. Used as a quizzer, it is the cheapest personal tutor ever available.

Two uncertainties should shape how hard you lean on any of this. The spacing ratios, FSRS efficiency claims and procedural-skill recommendations are extrapolations from students, medical trainees and lab word lists, and several key figures (marked †) have not been checked against full text. The lifestyle findings are also asymmetric. Sleep loss reliably hurts memory, but there is little evidence that healthy midlife adults can push memory *above* baseline with diet, supplements or stimulation. The sensible posture is therefore to protect against losses (sleep, stress at retrieval, AI over-reliance), invest in the two techniques with decades of replication, and treat anything sold as an enhancer as unproven until a preregistered trial in people your age says otherwise.
