# Supplements, Nootropics, Smart Drugs, Brain-Training and tDCS for Memory in Healthy Adults (30s–50s)

Scope note: almost all large, long trials were done in older adults (60–96 y). Direct evidence in healthy 30–50-year-olds is thin for nearly every intervention below, so most conclusions are extrapolated. This is not medical advice. Anyone on anticoagulants, psychiatric drugs or cardiovascular drugs, anyone pregnant, and anyone considering prescription stimulants or modafinil should talk to a clinician first.

Evidence grade key used below: **A** = large RCTs or consistent meta-analyses showing a meaningful effect; **B** = small but consistent benefit, or a benefit limited to a subgroup; **C** = weak, inconsistent, small or short trials; **N** = good evidence of no benefit; **M** = mainly marketing, or enforcement action taken.

## Supplements: omega-3/DHA, ginkgo (GEM), Bacopa, creatine, caffeine + L-theanine, B vitamins, vitamin D, Lion's Mane, Prevagen, multivitamins (COSMOS)

### Takeaway
No supplement has strong evidence of meaningfully improving memory in healthy midlife adults. The best signals are small. Multivitamins help episodic memory modestly in older adults (COSMOS; grade B for people 60+, untested in midlife). Creatine has a small pooled effect, SMD about 0.3, that is clearest in older or diseased people. Bacopa produces small, slow gains in free recall after 12 weeks or more, with publication-bias concerns. B vitamins show a very small pooled effect. Ginkgo (GEM trial) and vitamin D (VITAL) are clear nulls. Omega-3 is null in cognitively healthy people. Lion's Mane has only pilot data. Prevagen is the clearest marketing case and lost in court. Caffeine plus L-theanine helps attention and alertness acutely, not memory.

### Cited Findings
**Multivitamins (COSMOS), grade B for older adults; one industry-related caveat noted in Gaps**
- COSMOS-Mind (n=2,262, telephone testing, 3 y): multivitamin-mineral supplementation benefited global cognition, memory and executive function. Overall it did not reduce the risk of MCI or dementia (110 incident MCI and 14 incident dementia cases were adjudicated). — [PMC11103094](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11103094/); [PubMed 37035889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37035889/)
- COSMOS-Web (n≈3,562, online testing, older adults): the multivitamin significantly improved ModRey immediate recall at 1 y, the primary endpoint. The authors estimated the benefit as about 3.1 years of age-related memory change. Secondary outcomes were null. — [Yeung et al., PubMed 37244291](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37244291/); [Columbia news](https://news.columbia.edu/news/multivitamin-improves-memory-older-adults-study-finds)
- COSMOS-Clinic (n=573) found a favourable 2-year change in episodic memory. A 2024 meta-analysis of the three substudies found clear benefit for global cognition and episodic memory. — [PubMed 38244989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38244989/)
- Counterpoint: the Alzheimer's Association was not ready to recommend widespread multivitamin use, and Physicians' Health Study II (male physicians) found no cognitive benefit. — [PMC10375458](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10375458/)

**Creatine, grade B/C (small effect; younger healthy adults show roughly none)**
- Xu et al. 2024, Frontiers in Nutrition: 16 RCTs, n=492, a mix of healthy and diseased adults. Memory SMD = 0.31, certainty of evidence moderate. The benefit was larger in people with disease. — [PubMed 39070254](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39070254/); [PMC11275561](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11275561/)
- Prokopidis et al. 2022, Nutrition Reviews, healthy participants only (8 RCTs in the meta-analysis): memory SMD = 0.29. The effect was largest in adults aged 66–76 and essentially absent in younger participants. — [PubMed 35984306](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35984306/); [PMC9999677](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9999677/)

**Bacopa monnieri, grade C/B (small memory-specific effect; needs at least 12 weeks; possible publication bias)**
- Kongkeaw et al. 2014 meta-analysis (n=437, trials of 12 weeks or more): Trail B shortened by 17.9 ms (95% CI −24.6 to −11.2), and choice reaction time fell by 10.6 ms. — [PubMed 24252493](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24252493/)
- A 2012 systematic review found Bacopa improved 9 of 17 memory free-recall tests and showed little evidence in other domains. — [PubMed 22747190](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22747190/)
- A review of 11 RCTs in healthy people (n=645) found possible small gains in delayed recall, learning rate, digit span and logical memory, with considerable risk of publication bias. — [Examine summary](https://examine.com/summaries/study/7dxWZ9/)
- A 2026 network meta-analysis in Phytomedicine (29 RCTs, n=2,107 healthy adults) said Bacopa (Brahmi), especially at high doses, "shows promise" relative to ginkgo. Its authors noted that the lack of head-to-head trials limits the evidence. — [PubMed 41678913](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41678913/)

**Ginkgo biloba (GEM trial), grade N**
- GEM (Snitz et al., JAMA 2009;302:2663): 3,069 community-dwelling adults aged 72–96 took 120 mg twice daily for a median of about 6 years. There was no difference from placebo in annual decline rates in any domain, including memory, and no subgroup effects by age, sex, education or baseline cognition. — [PMC2832285](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2832285); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/12/091229164938.htm)
- GEM also found no reduction in dementia incidence (the NIH/NCCAM summary of the GEM primary outcome). — [NIH archived release](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20090119082639/http://www.nih.gov/news/health/nov2008/nccam-18.htm)

**Omega-3/DHA, grade N for cognitively healthy people; C for people with mild memory complaints**
- Cochrane (cognitively healthy older people): no benefit of omega-3 PUFA on cognitive function. Searches ran only to 2012. — [Cochrane CD005379.pub3](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD005379.pub3//ta)
- Yurko-Mauro 2015, PLoS One: more than 1 g/day DHA/EPA improved episodic memory, mainly in older adults with mild memory complaints. The lead author is from DSM/Martek, a DHA manufacturer, so treat this as industry-linked (see Gaps). — [PubMed 25786262](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25786262/)
- A 2015 J Psychopharmacol meta-analysis found only "marginal evidence" of benefit, limited to n-3-deficient people. — [PubMed 26040902](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26040902/)
- A 2020 meta-analysis found that the evidence does not support DHA for memory, executive function, attention or working memory in age-related decline. — [PubMed 32060571](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32060571/)

**B vitamins, grade C (very small effect; likely depends on baseline status or homocysteine)**
- A 2020 meta-analysis of non-demented adults found that B vitamins taken for more than 3 months helped global cognition (SMD −0.18, 95% CI −0.30 to −0.06) and episodic memory (SMD −0.09, 95% CI −0.15 to −0.04). The negative sign is the authors' convention for benefit. Study populations varied in their share of people with MCI. — [PMC7231132](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7231132/)

**Vitamin D, grade N**
- The same 2020 meta-analysis found vitamin D gave no benefit (SMD −0.06, 95% CI −0.36 to 0.23). — [PMC7231132](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7231132/)
- VITAL ancillary studies (about 4,200 adults aged 60+, 2,000 IU/day D3, 2–3 y): no effect on cognitive decline. An exploratory signal appeared in Black participants. — [PMC8636504](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8636504/)

**Caffeine + L-theanine, grade B for acute attention and alertness; C/none for memory**
- Camfield 2014, Nutr Rev (11 studies): moderate effects in the first 2 h after dosing, mainly on self-rated alertness and attention-switching accuracy. — [PubMed 24946991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24946991/)
- Payne et al. 2024, Proc Nutr Soc (50 RCTs, 15 meta-analysed): the combination improved choice reaction time and alertness at 1–2 h. The authors flag doses above tea-equivalent levels, few studies per analysis and wide confidence intervals. The memory evidence is weak. — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-nutrition-society/article/effects-of-tea-camellia-sinensis-or-its-bioactive-compounds-ltheanine-or-ltheanine-plus-caffeine-on-cognition-sleep-and-mood-in-healthy-participants-a-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis-of-randomised-controlled-trials/1F0ABB804FC3E74DCC0799E0403627DD)
- A 2022 systematic review covered caffeine and L-theanine together. — [PubMed 35111479](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35111479/)

**Lion's Mane (Hericium erinaceus), grade C (pilot data only)**
- Acute trial, 2025 (n=18, aged 18–35): no effect on global cognition or mood composites. The only gain was on a pegboard test at 90 min. — [PMC12018234](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12018234/)
- 28-day pilot, 2023 (about 40 people aged 18–45, 1.8 g/day): faster Stroop at 60 min (p=0.005). There were also null and some negative findings, and the authors urged caution. — [PMC10675414](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10675414/)
- A larger trial in healthy adults (n=87) is registered with no results yet. — [NCT07405957](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07405957)

**Prevagen (apoaequorin), grade M; federal FDA warning and FTC/NY AG enforcement**
- The company's Madison Memory Study (n=218, aged 40–91, 90 days, 9 computerized tests) showed no significant difference from placebo on any test. Per the FTC complaint, the company then analysed the data "more than 30 different ways" post hoc to find a subgroup. — [ConsumerLab](https://www.consumerlab.com/answers/does-prevagen-really-improve-memory/prevagen_memory/); [SupplySide](https://supplysidesj.com/claims/ftc-ag-schneiderman-target-prevagen-memory-supplement)
- The October 2012 FDA warning letter classed Prevagen as an unapproved new drug and cited failure to report adverse events and complaints. — [FDA warning letter (TINA copy)](https://truthinadvertising.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Prevagen-FDA-warning-letter.pdf)
- FTC and NY AG v. Quincy Bioscience (filed 2017, SDNY): in March 2024 a jury found the claims that Prevagen "reduces memory problems associated with aging" and is "clinically shown" to do so violated NY GBL §§349–350. Six other challenged statements were found not materially misleading. Claims against co-founder Mark Underwood were dismissed. — [NY AG](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2024/attorney-general-james-wins-trial-against-quincy-bioscience-deceptive-and); [TINA](https://truthinadvertising.org/articles/jury-hands-down-a-verdict-in-prevagen-deceptive-marketing-case)
- The court's order and judgment (2024) enjoins the claims, and the injunction applies nationally wherever Prevagen is marketed. — [FTC order and judgment](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/quincy_order_and_judgment.pdf); [Frankfurt Kurnit "brain claims enjoined"](https://advertisinglaw.fkks.com/post/102js9h/brain-claims-enjoined); [TINA Aug 29 2024 decision](https://truthinadvertising.org/brands/prevagen/ftc-v-qb_aug-29-2024-decision)

### Inferences
- Pooled effects of about 0.1–0.3 SD are roughly at the edge of what a person would notice day to day. For a healthy 40-year-old, "statistically significant" does not mean "felt."
- Positive results cluster in older people and people with deficiencies or disease, which suggests that correcting a deficit works better than enhancing a normal brain. For midlife adults, testing for B12 or vitamin D deficiency and correcting it is more rational than blanket supplementation.
- Prevagen is the clearest example of the post-hoc-subgroup marketing pattern. Consumers should be wary of "clinically shown" claims backed by a single company-run trial.

### Gaps
- Industry funding of COSMOS: Pfizer Consumer Healthcare (now Haleon) donated the multivitamins and Mars Edge supported the cocoa arm. This is from my prior knowledge and was not verified in this session.
- The Yurko-Mauro (2015) DSM/Martek affiliation is from prior knowledge and should be verified in the paper's COI statement.
- Safety notes I could not source in this session, so verify them via NIH ODS or NCCIH: ginkgo bleeding risk and interactions with anticoagulants or antiplatelets; Bacopa GI side effects (nausea, cramps); possible bradycardia and thyroid interactions with Bacopa; creatine water weight and kidney concerns in people with renal disease; high-dose B6 neuropathy; vitamin D toxicity at very high doses; caffeine effects on sleep and anxiety; contamination and mislabelling of supplements, which FDA does not pre-approve.
- No large RCT of any of these supplements specifically enrols healthy people aged 30–50.
- I found no confirmation of the final monetary relief in the Prevagen case or of any appeal status after the 2024 judgment.
- I found no pooled standardised effect size for Bacopa in healthy adults.

## Prescription "smart drugs" (modafinil, stimulants) in healthy non-ADHD adults

### Takeaway
In rested healthy adults, modafinil's overall cognitive effect is tiny (g≈0.10), and memory effects are inconsistent. Methylphenidate and amphetamine give small gains in inhibitory control and some episodic-memory consolidation, but publication bias inflates these. Users may mainly feel more motivated rather than becoming more capable. Risks include sleep disruption, next-day working-memory decrements, dependence and cardiovascular effects. Non-prescribed use is illegal (Schedule II stimulants; modafinil is Schedule IV). Grade C for memory benefit, with real risk.

### Cited Findings
- Battleday & Brem 2015 systematic review (healthy, not sleep-deprived, 1990–2014): only about half of basic-paradigm studies showed learning or memory gains. Complex tasks showed more consistent gains in attention, executive function and learning. — [PubMed 26381811](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26381811/); [Oxford news](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2015-08-20-review-‘smart-drug’-shows-modafinil-does-enhance-cognition)
- Kredlow et al. 2019 meta-analysis (19 placebo-controlled trials): the overall effect across domains was small, g = 0.10 (95% CI 0.05–0.15). The authors saw "limited potential" outside sleep-deprived populations. — [PubMed 31433334](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31433334/)
- Roberts et al. 2020 meta-analyses of acute modafinil, methylphenidate and d-amphetamine in healthy adults: they summarised earlier work showing that methylphenidate improved memory while modafinil only improved attention in people who were not sleep-deprived. — [Roberts 2020 PDF (LJMU)](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/13268/8/How%20effective%20are%20pharmaceuticals%20for%20cognitive%20enhancement%20in%20healthy%20adults%20A%20series%20of%20meta-analyses%20of%20cognitive%20performance%20during%20acute%20administration%20of%20modafinil,%20methylphenidate%20and%20d-amphetamine..pdf)
- Ilieva, Hook & Farah 2015 (48 studies, n=1,409, methylphenidate and amphetamine): small effects on inhibitory control and short-term episodic memory, and medium effects on delayed episodic memory. Publication bias led the authors to judge the effect "probably modest." Healthy users may seek energy and motivation more than cognition. — [PubMed 25591060](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25591060/)
- Risks: a UC Irvine study found healthy adults on dextroamphetamine performed worse on working memory 24 h later, after sleep, and that sleep was disturbed. — [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/326023.php)
- A 2021 systematic review of students lists tolerance, dependence, and cardiovascular and neurological complications. — [Brain Sci 2021 (Herts)](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/8953/1/brainsci_11_00355_v2.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a healthy 30–50-year-old, the expected memory gain from these drugs is smaller than the gain from sleeping well. The drugs can also impair the sleep that consolidates memory, so the net effect could be negative.
- Clinician consultation is essential: these are controlled substances with cardiac, psychiatric and dependence risks.

### Gaps
- No long-term RCT data exist on chronic off-label use in healthy adults.
- I did not source in-session the modafinil-specific risks: rare serious rash (SJS/TEN), its interaction with hormonal contraceptives, and its Schedule IV status. Verify these from the FDA label.

## Commercial brain-training (Lumosity FTC 2016, Simons 2016, ACTIVE, n-back/WM transfer meta-analyses)

### Takeaway
Training reliably improves the trained task, near transfer is modest, and far transfer to everyday memory or IQ is near zero when active controls are used. The FTC fined Lumosity in 2016 for unsupported claims. ACTIVE's 2026 20-year follow-up links speed-of-processing training with boosters to 25% lower claims-based dementia incidence in older adults. The memory training arm showed no effect on dementia. The speed-training software is now sold commercially, which is a conflict of interest. Grade: M for broad "brain-boost" claims; N for far transfer; B/C for speed training's dementia signal in people 65+.

### Cited Findings
- FTC v. Lumos Labs (January 2016): $2M redress, with a $50M judgment suspended because of the company's finances. Lumos had to notify auto-renew subscribers from 2009–2014 and let them cancel. Its claims had included better performance at work and school and protection against MCI, dementia and Alzheimer's. The company neither admitted nor denied the charges. This was the first government settlement with a brain-training app maker. — [FTC press release](https://ftc.gov/news-events/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges); [NBC](https://www.nbcnews.com/business/consumer/lumosity-pay-2m-settle-ftc-charges-over-brain-training-ads-n490571)
- Simons et al. 2016, PSPI 17(3):103–186: the review evaluated the studies cited by leading brain-training companies. It found extensive evidence for improvement on trained tasks, less for closely related tasks and little for distant skills. Evidence for real-world benefit was "inadequate." The authors recommended preregistration, active controls and COI disclosure. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/brain-training.html); [Illinois news](https://news.illinois.edu/review-finds-little-evidence-that-brain-training-games-yield-real-world-benefits/)
- Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme 2016, Perspect Psychol Sci (87 publications, 145 comparisons): no convincing far transfer against treated controls, and "no evidential value" in studies using treated controls. — [PMC4968033](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4968033); [ORA Oxford](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:daa9a729-f17b-4e45-97c0-cff5075ea404)
- Sala & Gobet: in a WM-training meta-analysis in children (2017), near transfer was g=0.46 and far transfer g=0.12. A second-order meta-analysis found near transfer g≈0.27 and far transfer g≈0.12, approaching zero once study quality is accounted for. — [Sala 2017 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/dual-n-back/2017-sala.pdf); [Liverpool repository](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3006842/)
- ACTIVE 20-year follow-up (Coe et al. 2026, Alzheimer's & Dementia: TRCI, doi 10.1002/trc2.70197): using Medicare claims for 2,021 of the original participants (72%), 1999–2019, dementia was diagnosed in 40% (105/264) of the speed-plus-booster group and 49% (239/491) of controls, a 25% reduction. There was no effect without boosters and no significant effect for memory or reasoning training. Diagnoses came from claims, not from cognitive testing. — [Johns Hopkins](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/news/newsroom/news-releases/2026/02/cognitive-speed-training-linked-to-lower-dementia-incidence-up-to-20-years-later); [UF Health](https://ufhealth.org/news/2026/decades-later-brain-training-lowers-dementia-risk)
- COI: Posit Science acquired and markets the speed-of-processing and UFOV software used in ACTIVE, now sold as BrainHQ. — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1115860)

### Inferences
- The ACTIVE finding concerns dementia risk in people 65+, from a small booster subgroup, and is not memory enhancement in midlife. It is a single trial's secondary, claims-based outcome and should not be marketed as "brain training prevents dementia."
- For a 30–50-year-old, an app will make you better at the app. Learning real skills, exercise and sleep are better-supported routes, though those are outside this note's scope.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch Sala & Gobet's adult-specific WM/n-back meta-analyses (for example, the 2019/2020 multilevel analyses).
- I did not review the 2014 Stanford "consensus" letter, the rebuttal letter from about 133 scientists, or other FTC brain-training actions (for example, LearningRx and Carrot Neurotechnology in 2016).

## Neurostimulation (consumer tDCS)

### Takeaway
The evidence is contested and generally weak for memory in healthy adults. The most-cited meta-analysis (Horvath 2015) found no reliable single-session cognitive effect. Later meta-analyses find at most small effects, for example faster but not more accurate responses. Consumer devices are not validated for memory enhancement. Grade C/N.

### Cited Findings
- Horvath, Forte & Carter 2015, Brain Stimulation: among healthy adults, restricted to effects with at least one independent replication, there was no significant active-versus-sham difference on memory, language or arithmetic tasks. — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/brain-hackers-beware-scientist-says-tdcs-has-no-effect); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/trouble-tdcs-electrical-brain-stimulation-may-not-work-after-all)
- Critics such as Mancuso et al. argue earlier meta-analyses underestimated working-memory effects. — [Mancuso WM meta-analysis PDF](https://neuroethics.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/tDCS-WM-meta-analysis.pdf)
- A single-session anodal DLPFC tDCS meta-analysis found faster but not more accurate responses in healthy people. An umbrella review of prefrontal tDCS meta-analyses cites null net effects in healthy people. — [PMC8018377](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8018377); [PMC8866814](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8866814)
- A 2024 Bayesian meta-regression in healthy young adults covered 721 effects from 106 reports. I did not verify its conclusions. — [Frontiers Hum Neurosci 2024](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2024.1305446/full)

### Inferences
- Lab protocols with precise montages do not translate reliably to DIY or consumer use. High inter-individual variability makes "it works for me" anecdotes unreliable.

### Gaps
- FDA regulatory status of consumer tDCS headsets (for example, whether they are sold as wellness devices) was not sourced this session.
- Safety specifics (skin burns, headache, unknown long-term effects of repeated DIY use) were not sourced. Verify them before reporting.
