# Evidence-Based Learning and Encoding Techniques for Long-Term Retention in Adults

Research notes compiled 2026-11-25. Access note: full texts on doi.org, frontiersin.org and minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au could not be fetched (these hosts are blocked by this session's network settings). Most figures below come from abstracts, publisher pages and repository records shown in search results. Effect sizes should be checked against the original papers before final publication. The evidence grades are the researcher's own judgments: **A** means several meta-analyses agree, **B** means one meta-analysis or many replicated experiments, **C** means mixed results or few studies, **D** means weak, failed or not supported.

## Q1. What do the major reviews conclude about retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, elaboration, self-explanation, dual coding, concrete examples and generation?

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice (practice testing) and spaced practice are the most robust techniques. Several meta-analyses give them medium to large effects (g ≈ 0.5–0.75), and they are the only two techniques Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated "high utility." Self-explanation (g ≈ 0.55), generation (≈0.40) and interleaving (g ≈ 0.42, mainly for similar categories and math, and not for text or word lists) have moderate support that depends on conditions. Dual coding and concrete examples were not covered by the meta-analyses retrieved for these notes.

### Cited Findings
**Dunlosky et al. (2013), PSPI 14(1):4–58. This is the anchor review.**
- It reviewed 10 techniques: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, summarization, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text learning, rereading, practice testing, distributed practice and interleaved practice — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/954654); [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
- **High utility:** practice testing and distributed practice. They benefited learners across ages and ability levels and across many content areas — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [Kent State](https://www.kent.edu/kent/news/success/all-study-strategies-not-created-equal-according-kent-state-researchers)
- **Moderate utility:** elaborative interrogation (explaining *why* a stated fact is true) is confirmed in the retrieved sources — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html). (Self-explanation and interleaved practice are widely reported as the other two moderate ratings. This was not confirmed in the retrieved snippets; see Gaps.)
- **Low utility:** summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text learning and rereading — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)

**Donoghue & Hattie (2021), Frontiers in Education. This is a quantitative meta-analysis of Dunlosky's 10 techniques.**
- It covered 242 studies, 1,619 effects and 169,179 participants. The overall mean effect is 0.56. Distributed practice and practice testing were the most effective. Underlining and summarization were the least effective, though they still had "relatively high effects" — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/3193f29b450640339dea0c3bade391de); [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/text)
- Moderators were feedback, near vs. far transfer and learner ability. Effects were much larger for lower-ability students. Most studies measured surface or factual outcomes, so the authors urge caution about deeper, relational learning — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/text)
- Per-technique effect sizes could not be retrieved (full text blocked).

**Retrieval practice / testing effect (Grade A)**
- Rowland (2014), *Psychological Bulletin* 140(6):1432–1463, compared testing with restudy across 61 studies. The mean effect is g ≈ 0.50. Recall-type initial tests gave larger benefits than recognition tests, which supports an effortful-retrieval account. Support for semantic-elaboration accounts was limited — [PubMed 25150680](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/); [PMC review citing g=0.50](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703)
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), *Review of Educational Research* 87(3):659–701, covered 118 articles, 272 effect sizes and 15,472 participants. Practice tests beat restudying *and all other comparison conditions*. Test features, participants and outcomes moderated the effects. Results on feedback were mixed: in some comparisons, feedback did not significantly increase the testing effect — [WSU PDF](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/); [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/2/9-1). The overall g is reported as about 0.70 in a secondary review — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703). The often-quoted g = 0.61 could not be verified; see Gaps.
- Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt (2021), *Educational Psychology Review* 33:1409–1453, reviewed about 50 classroom experiments with 49 effect sizes and more than 5,000 students. 57% of effects were medium or large. Settings were 40% secondary, 40% undergraduate and 20% medical school — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9); [retrievalpractice.org](https://retrievalpractice.org/review); [Tes](https://www.tes.com/magazine/teaching-learning/general/why-retrieval-practice-safe-bet-teachers)

**Spaced / distributed practice (Grade A)**
- Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021), *Educational Psychology Review*, meta-analysed 29 studies. Spaced retrieval beat massed retrieval (g = 0.74). Expanding schedules did **not** beat uniform ones (g = 0.034, ns). An expanding schedule helps more as the number of tests per item grows — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)
- Cepeda et al. (2008), *Psychological Science* 19(11), studied more than 1,350 participants with gaps of up to 3.5 months and tests up to 1 year later. Lengthening the gap first improves and then slowly reduces final recall, a non-monotonic "ridgeline." The optimal gap is about 20–40% of a 1-week retention interval and about 5–10% of a 1-year interval — [PubMed 19076480](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/); [eScholarship preprint](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0kp5q19x/qt0kp5q19x.pdf?t=lnry4n)
- Cepeda et al. (2006), "Optimizing distributed practice," is the earlier theoretical and practical synthesis — [USF Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1764)

**Interleaving (Grade B, but conditional)**
- Brunmair & Richter (2019), *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11):1029–1052, is a multilevel meta-analysis. The overall effect is g = 0.42, with substantial heterogeneity. Math tasks show g = 0.34. Effects on expository texts and word lists were not significant. Effects are stronger when categories are **similar to each other** (high between-category similarity), when items within a category are less similar, and when material is more complex — [PubMed 31556629](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/); [author PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)

**Self-explanation (Grade B)**
- Bisra et al. (2018), *Educational Psychology Review*, pooled 64 reports and 69 effects. The random-effects mean is g = 0.55 (95% CI 0.45–0.65). Prompted self-explanation outperformed explanations given by an instructor — [Springer DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies)

**Generation effect (Grade B)**
- Bertsch et al. (2007), *Memory & Cognition*, covered 86 studies and 445 effect sizes. The mean effect is about 0.40, roughly half a standard deviation, for generating rather than reading — [PubMed 17645161](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/); [author PDF](https://mcdaniel97.github.io/Publications/Bertsch%20et%20al.%202007.pdf)

### Inferences
- In rough order of evidence strength: retrieval practice ≈ spacing (A) > self-explanation ≈ generation (B) > interleaving (B but conditional) > elaborative interrogation (moderate, mostly fact learning). Spaced retrieval combines the top two and shows the largest pooled effect found here (g = 0.74).
- Rowland's recall > recognition finding suggests that free-recall or short-answer self-tests should be preferred to multiple-choice re-recognition.
- Dunlosky's "low utility" ratings mean low *relative* utility. Donoghue & Hattie still found non-trivial effects for these techniques, likely because the comparison groups were often doing nothing or plain reading. Low-utility techniques beat nothing, but they cost time that could go to better techniques.

### Gaps
- Per-technique d values from Donoghue & Hattie (2021) could not be retrieved because the full text was blocked.
- The Adesope (2017) overall effect: a secondary source gives g ≈ 0.70, and g = 0.61 is often quoted. Neither was confirmed against the primary text, so check the paper.
- Dunlosky's moderate ratings for self-explanation and interleaving are widely cited but were not confirmed in the retrieved snippets.
- **Dual coding and concrete examples:** no meta-analysis was retrieved in this session. Dunlosky rated "imagery for text learning" low utility, which is related but is not the same as dual coding (pairing words with diagrams). The Learning Scientists promote both techniques, but support comes mostly from multimedia-learning research (Mayer) that was not reviewed here. Treat these as plausible but not graded by this researcher.

## Q2. What spacing intervals are optimal for a given retention horizon?

### Takeaway
The optimal gap between study sessions grows with how long you need to remember the material, but it shrinks as a *fraction* of that time. It is about 20–40% of a 1-week horizon and about 5–10% of a 1-year horizon. Gaps that are too long cost much less than gaps that are too short. Expanding schedules are not reliably better than evenly spaced ones.

### Cited Findings
- Optimal gap ≈ 20–40% of the retention interval at 1 week and ≈ 5–10% at 1 year. Performance falls off gently past the optimum, so overshooting costs less than undershooting — [Cepeda et al. 2008, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/); [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0kp5q19x/qt0kp5q19x.pdf?t=lnry4n)
- Before this study, only about a dozen spacing studies used retention intervals of at least 1 day, and only a handful went beyond 1 week. Long-horizon evidence is therefore thinner than the lab literature as a whole — [eScholarship preprint](https://escholarship.org/content/qt0kp5q19x/qt0kp5q19x.pdf?t=lnry4n)
- Expanding vs. uniform schedules: g = 0.034, not significant. Expanding schedules gain an edge as tests per item increase — [Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)
- Bjork's theory gives the mechanism. The lower an item's current retrieval strength, the larger the gain in storage strength from successfully retrieving or restudying it. Some forgetting between sessions is therefore useful — [Bjork & Bjork 2020, JARMAC](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2021/01/RABjorkELBjorkJARMAC2020ForPostingSingleSpaced.pdf); [Bjork & Bjork 2011](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)

### Inferences (practical dosing, derived from the ratios above)
| Need to remember for | First review gap (approx.) |
|---|---|
| 1 week (e.g., exam next week) | 1–2 days |
| 1 month | ~1 week |
| 3 months | ~2–3 weeks |
| 1 year+ (professional knowledge) | ~3–5 weeks, then keep reviewing |
- For indefinite professional retention, reviewing items as their recall becomes uncertain (as SRS software such as Anki does) puts the ridgeline into practice. Several retrievals per item probably matter more than whether the schedule expands. A sensible default is either expanding or evenly spaced reviews, with *3+ successful spaced retrievals* per item.
- Re-studying after a failed retrieval (feedback) is a sensible default, even though Adesope reported mixed feedback effects.

### Gaps
- The ratio figures come from one large study (Cepeda 2008) and its 2009 companion. No independent replication of those exact ratios was found in this session.
- Most spacing studies use verbal or fact material. Optimal gaps for complex procedural skills such as SQL are not established.

## Q3. What is the evidence for mnemonics, especially the method of loci (memory palace)?

### Takeaway
Method-of-loci training produces large, lasting gains in memorizing lists in ordinary adults. Dresler et al. (2017) found that 6 weeks of about 30 min/day roughly doubled word-list recall, with gains lasting 4 months. The technique is best suited to arbitrary, ordered material (lists, sequences, numbers). It does little for understanding, and the keyword mnemonic was rated low utility by Dunlosky.

### Cited Findings
- Dresler et al. (2017), *Neuron*, compared 23 memory athletes (top 50 at the World Memory Championships) with 51 naive controls split into three groups: method-of-loci training, working-memory training, or no training. The loci group trained about 30 min/day online for 6 weeks — [Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2017/03/memorization-tool-bulks-up-brains-internal-connections.html); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/dont-forget-you-too-can-acquire-a-super-memory/)
- Word-list recall: reports range from 26 → 62 words to "+35 words from a baseline of about 30." Secondary sources differ, but all describe roughly a doubling. Gains persisted at 4 months. Resting-state brain connectivity shifted toward the pattern seen in athletes — [Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2017/03/memorization-tool-bulks-up-brains-internal-connections.html); [Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/you-can-become-a-memory-champion-too-11671); [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/za/blog/brain-waves/201703/how-train-your-brain-memory-champion)
- A later eLife reviewed preprint reanalyses the same datasets (n = 17 memory-training participants) — [eLife](https://elifesciences.org/reviewed-preprints/109943)
- The keyword mnemonic is rated **low utility** by Dunlosky et al. (2013) — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)

### Inferences
- The method is practical for ordered or arbitrary information: speech outlines, lists of regulatory sections, step sequences, acronym-free lists and number codes. It is impractical for conceptual or relational knowledge such as why a SQL join behaves a certain way or how financial rules interact, where self-explanation and retrieval do better.
- The setup cost is real: about 15+ hours of training before a novice becomes fluent. The cost is worthwhile only when a lot of arbitrary material must be memorized verbatim.
- The training sample was small (17–23 per group), so the size of the effect is uncertain even though its direction is clear.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of the method of loci was retrieved. A 2014 meta-analysis (Twomey & Kroneisen / Kroneisen et al.) and later work exist, but they were not verified in this session.
- No evidence was found that loci-trained individuals transfer the skill to work-relevant learning.

## Q4. What is the evidence on low-utility methods: rereading, highlighting, summarizing and learning styles?

### Takeaway
Rereading, highlighting and summarizing are the most popular study habits, yet Dunlosky rated them low utility. They give modest gains over doing nothing but are clearly worse than retrieval practice. Learning-styles "meshing" (matching teaching to a preferred modality) has no adequate evidence base and is considered a neuromyth.

### Cited Findings
- Rereading and highlighting are among students' most common habits, but they are rated low utility. Summarization is also rated low — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
- In a quantitative meta-analysis, underlining and summarization were the least effective of the 10 techniques, though they still had "relatively high effects" against weak comparison conditions — [Donoghue & Hattie 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/text)
- Practice testing beat restudying in 61 studies (g ≈ 0.50), which is a direct comparison against rereading — [Rowland 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/)
- Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008), PSPI 9(3):105–119, concluded that few studies used the crossover design needed to test the meshing hypothesis. Several that did "flatly contradict" it, and there is "no adequate evidence base" for using learning-styles assessments in education — [PubMed 26162104](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162104/); [USF](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/)
- Later reviews describe meshing as a persistent neuromyth — [Frontiers in Education 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389%2Ffeduc.2023.1147498); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6113575/)
- Bjork's mechanism explains why rereading feels effective: easy, fluent study raises current retrieval strength without building storage strength, so performance during study is misleading — [Psychology Today (Bjork-based)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/all-about-addiction/201105/desirable-difficulties-in-the-classroom/amp); [Bjork & Bjork 2011](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rereading is "low utility," not "zero utility." One reading for comprehension is necessary. *Repeated* rereading in place of self-testing is the problem.
- Summarizing can work better if it is done from memory, which turns it into retrieval or generation. Dunlosky's low rating reflects variable quality and the need for training.
- People do have modality *preferences*. What lacks evidence is that matching instruction to those preferences improves learning.

### Gaps
- Separate pooled effect sizes for rereading and highlighting could not be retrieved.

## Q5. How do these techniques apply to adult professionals learning technical or procedural material (SQL, regulations, financial rules) vs. declarative facts? Transfer and desirable difficulties.

### Takeaway
Testing effects do transfer to new questions and applications, but by less than they help with the tested material itself (d ≈ 0.40 overall). Transfer is strongest for application and inference questions and weakest for rearranged item pairs. Desirable difficulties (spacing, interleaving, retrieval, variation, generation) slow down how fast learners seem to improve but strengthen retention and transfer. For procedural skills, interleaving similar problem types is the most relevant technique.

### Cited Findings
- Pan & Rickard (2018), *Psychological Bulletin* 144(7):710–756, analysed 192 effect sizes. The overall transfer effect is d ≈ 0.40. It rises to about 0.58 for transfer across test formats. Transfer is greatest to new test formats, application and inference questions, medical-diagnosis problems and mediator or related cues. It is weakest to rearranged stimulus-response items — [author PDF](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018.pdf); [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zC1oBp6yE72b7YHzaJZmjXf)
- Interleaving works best when categories are similar and easily confused and when material is complex. It is not significant for expository text or word lists — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)
- Desirable difficulties work because they "trigger encoding and retrieval processes" that support learning. Undesirable difficulties do not, because learners cannot overcome them — [Bjork & Bjork 2020](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2021/01/RABjorkELBjorkJARMAC2020ForPostingSingleSpaced.pdf); [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/desirable-difficulties)
- Current performance reflects retrieval strength, while storage strength slows forgetting and speeds relearning — [Bjork & Bjork 2011](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)
- Meta-analytic evidence is mostly from studies with surface or factual outcomes, so caution is needed for deeper, relational learning — [Donoghue & Hattie 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/text)
- Self-explanation prompts beat instructor explanations (g = 0.55), which is relevant to understanding rules and procedures — [Bisra 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)
- Classroom evidence includes 20% medical-school samples, a professional-adjacent population — [Agarwal 2021](https://retrievalpractice.org/review)

### Inferences (practical protocols; researcher's synthesis, not directly tested)
- **Declarative facts** (regulatory thresholds, deadlines, account codes): use spaced-retrieval flashcards with recall rather than recognition, aiming for 3+ spaced successful recalls. Add elaborative interrogation ("why is this threshold $X?") to tie each fact to its rationale.
- **Procedural or technical material (SQL)**: practise retrieval by *producing* the output (write the query from a prompt; don't recognise it). Interleave similar, easily confused problem types (e.g., LEFT vs. INNER JOIN, WHERE vs. HAVING, window functions vs. GROUP BY), since interleaving helps most when categories are similar. Self-explain each worked example. Space the practice sets.
- **Rules and regulations needing application**: test with scenario or application questions rather than verbatim recall, because Pan & Rickard show the largest transfer to application and inference items and across formats. Vary the scenarios (variation is a desirable difficulty).
- Expect practice to *feel* worse and early scores to be lower. Judge progress by delayed tests, not by how fluent study feels.
- Adults with more prior knowledge may gain less, since Donoghue & Hattie found larger effects for lower-ability learners. This is an inference; adult professionals were not studied directly.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis specific to adult workplace learners or to technical skills like SQL was found. Applying these findings to such learners is an extrapolation from student and lab populations.
- No meta-analysis on worked examples or the expertise-reversal effect was retrieved, though both are relevant to procedural learning.

## Q6. Note-taking: longhand vs. laptop (Mueller & Oppenheimer 2014 and its replications)

### Takeaway
The widely publicised advantage of longhand notes (Mueller & Oppenheimer 2014, N = 67) **did not replicate**. Two direct replications (Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson 2019; Urry et al. 2021, N = 145, with an 8-study mini-meta-analysis) found small, non-significant differences. The robust finding is that laptop notes are more verbatim and longer. More words go with better scores, and more verbatim overlap goes with worse scores. Grade D for "handwriting is better," and the evidence should be treated as unsettled.

### Cited Findings
- The original study had 67 Princeton undergraduates. The replication had 145 Tufts undergraduates watching a recorded TED-style talk and included a mini meta-analysis of 8 studies. Quiz differences by medium were small and non-significant. Results "do not support the idea that longhand note taking improves immediate learning via better encoding" — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/no-benefit-handwriting-notes.html); [Psychological Science, Urry et al. 2021, 32(3):326–339](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797620965541/)
- Laptop users wrote more words and more verbatim content. Higher word count predicted better scores, and more verbatim overlap predicted worse scores. The authors caution that these may stand in for motivation or conscientiousness — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/no-benefit-handwriting-notes.html)
- There was no relationship between medium and conceptual recall, unlike in the original — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/no-benefit-handwriting-notes.html)
- Limitations: no chance to review notes before the quiz, and a TED talk rather than a real lecture. The medium might matter when learners study their notes later — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/no-benefit-handwriting-notes.html)
- Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson (2019), *Educational Psychology Review*, ran a direct replication with added eWriter and no-notes groups. Some trends favoured longhand, but performance did not differ consistently. A meta-analysis of direct replications found small, non-significant effects favouring longhand, and declaring one method superior is "premature" — [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007%2Fs10648-019-09468-2.pdf)

### Inferences
- Practical guidance: the medium matters less than the *process*. Paraphrase rather than transcribe, avoid digital distraction (a laptop risk not tested in these lab designs), and above all *use* the notes for retrieval practice later instead of rereading them.

### Gaps
- No large, preregistered study was found that tests delayed retention after reviewing notes by medium. The 2024 EEG handwriting-vs-typing studies were not reviewed here; they are neural-correlate studies, not retention outcomes.
