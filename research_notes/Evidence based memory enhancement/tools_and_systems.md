# Practical Tools and Knowledge-Management Systems for Retaining and Retrieving Work Knowledge

Note on method: these notes come from web searches and page fetches on 2026-11-25. docs.ankiweb.net and arxiv.org were blocked by the session's network settings, so the Anki FAQ and the MIT preprint abstract were read only through search snippets. Labels used below: **[RCT]** randomized experiment; **[Lab]** controlled lab experiment; **[Correlational]** survey or observational; **[Benchmark]** algorithm prediction benchmark; **[Practitioner]** expert opinion or anecdote; **[Preprint]** not peer-reviewed.

## 1. Spaced repetition software: algorithms, tools, card-writing, and evidence of professional use

### Takeaway
FSRS, the open-source scheduler built into Anki since late 2023 and also adopted by RemNote, predicts forgetting measurably better than older algorithms. Claims of "20–30% fewer reviews than SM-2" come from simulations and secondary sources, so treat them as a reasonable estimate, not a proven figure. The card-writing guidance from Wozniak and Matuschak is practitioner wisdom grounded in retrieval-practice research rather than tested rule by rule. Professional-use evidence is strongest among medical students, and it is correlational.

### Cited Findings
**FSRS benchmark (open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark) [Benchmark]**
- The benchmark uses Anki review logs from about 10k users. The README gives both "20k users / ~1.7B reviews" and "10k users / ~727M reviews." Evaluated sets: 9,999 collections and about 350M reviews without same-day reviews, or 10,000 collections and about 519M reviews with them. It uses a time-series split, training on older reviews and testing on newer ones. — [srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)
- Metrics: Log Loss and RMSE (bins) measure calibration, meaning whether predicted recall probability matches what actually happened. AUC measures discrimination, meaning how well a model separates recalls from lapses. — [srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)
- Results without same-day reviews (Log Loss / RMSE-bins / AUC): FSRS-6 0.3460 / 0.0653 / 0.7034; FSRS-5 0.3561 / 0.0742 / 0.7010; FSRS-4.5 0.3625 / 0.0764 / 0.6891; FSRS v4 0.3726 / 0.0838 / 0.6853; HLR (Duolingo's half-life regression) 0.4694 / 0.1275 / 0.6369; Ebisu v2 0.4989 / 0.1627 / 0.6051; constant-average baseline 0.3945 / 0.1034 / 0.4997. An experimental "FSRS-7 recency" variant scores 0.3363 / 0.0576 / 0.7243. — [srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark); [srs-benchmark wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark/wiki)
- A neural model (RWKV-Instant) has the best log loss (0.2773), so FSRS is not the theoretical ceiling. It is, however, the best lightweight, per-user-trainable scheduler that ships in consumer apps. — [srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)
- The README text does not give FSRS-vs-SM-2 numbers. Those appear in images and companion pages. A secondary guide says FSRS predicts recall better than SM-2 for 99.5% of users in the benchmark and estimates 20–30% fewer reviews, but the review-reduction figure is simulation-based. RemNote cites the same 20–30% range. — [Diane guide (secondary)](https://www.diane.app/en/guides/fsrs-vs-sm2); [RemNote help](https://help.remnote.com/en/articles/9124137-the-fsrs-spaced-repetition-algorithm); [Expertium benchmark page](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html)
- Anki's FAQ, via a search snippet, says FSRS users "have to do fewer reviews than with Anki's default algorithm to achieve the same retention level." — [Anki FAQ](https://faqs.ankiweb.net/what-spaced-repetition-algorithm)
- Caveat: SM-2 was never designed to output recall probabilities, so the benchmark has to add formulas to compare it. Some users report more reviews under FSRS at matched settings. — [AnkiWeb forum thread](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/has-anyone-done-a-live-comparison-of-fsrs-and-sm2-as-implemented-in-anki-it-looks-like-no-so-can-anyone-help-me-set-it-up/34996); [Anki SM2 beating FSRS in simulator (forum)](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/anki-sm2-beating-fsrs-in-fsrs-simulator/34866)

**Card-writing guidance [Practitioner, grounded in retrieval-practice research]**
- Wozniak's "Twenty Rules of Formulating Knowledge" (SuperMemo, 1999) lists rules in order of importance. Central rules: understand before you memorize, and the "minimum information principle," which means formulating each item as simply as possible so a single repetition retrieves minimal information. Cloze deletion (fill-in-the-blank) is recommended as the fastest way to turn text into items, especially for beginners. — [SuperMemo: 20 rules](https://www.supermemo.com/en/archives1990-2015/articles/20rules)
- Matuschak's "How to write good prompts" (2020) treats retrieval practice as the main mechanism. Prompts should be focused, precise, consistent, tractable, and effortful. Avoid leading and yes/no questions, keep prompts concise and context-independent, and write several prompts per idea so they collectively cover all key details. He notes that evidence "stops well short" of exact prompt-writing guidance. — [Matuschak, How to write good prompts](https://andymatuschak.org/prompts/); [Matuschak note: prompts should ensure retrieval](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zENFxxXLVZqyJz2qaXxuy2p); [Matuschak note: writing good prompts is hard](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zKy4FsHTcf8LdkgXkMueeGL)
- Matuschak also warns that people often don't notice their own prompts are bad. — [Matuschak note](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zKy4FsHTcf8LdkgXkMueeGL)

**Underlying evidence for retrieval practice [Meta-analyses]**
- Rowland (2014) meta-analysis: testing effect Hedges' g ≈ 0.50, lab-focused. Adesope et al. (2017, Review of Educational Research): 272 effect sizes, overall g ≈ 0.61–0.70 depending on source, and classroom g ≈ 0.67. Secondary sources disagree on the exact overall value. — [Frontiers in Education review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2019.00005/full); [WSU PDF on Adesope](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/); [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703)

**Professional use: medical students [Correlational]**
- Deng et al. (2015), Washington University: each extra ~1,700 unique Anki cards, or ~445 boards-style questions, was associated with about +1 point on USMLE Step 1 after controlling for MCAT and other factors. Practice questions gave more "points per item" than cards. — [Deng et al. 2015, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26498443/); [full text PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4673073)
- Later surveys: daily Anki use correlated with higher Step 1 but not Step 2 scores, and consistency of use mattered. One cohort comparison found a non-significant difference (223.71 vs 222.58). — [PMC8651966](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8651966); [PMC10176558](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10176558); [Med Sci Educ 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40670-023-01826-8)

### Inferences
- For a working professional, switching Anki to FSRS with desired retention around 0.85–0.90 is a low-cost, evidence-aligned default. The scheduler matters less than card quality and daily consistency.
- In the Deng data, practice questions yielded more per item than flashcards. For applied skills such as DAX, SQL, and FMR interpretation, problem-style cards and practice problems are probably worth more than definition cards.
- The tools differ mainly in workflow: Anki (free, FSRS, huge add-on ecosystem), RemNote (notes and cards in one, FSRS), Mochi (Markdown-based, simple), SuperMemo (Windows-only, incremental reading, proprietary SM-18+). The best tool is the one used daily.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the exact FSRS-vs-SM-2 "99.5% of users" figure or the 20–30% review reduction from primary text, because those charts are images and docs.ankiweb.net was blocked. They should be labeled secondary or simulated.
- I found no direct head-to-head benchmark of SuperMemo's current algorithm (SM-18/19) against FSRS. Expertium's companion repos reportedly hold this but were not fetched.
- I found no outcome studies on Mochi or RemNote. Their feature details come from general knowledge and were not verified this session.
- I found no published studies of SRS use by mid-career professionals outside medicine and language learning. Language-learning evidence (e.g., Duolingo HLR) concerns algorithm design, not workplace outcomes.

## 2. External memory / "second brain" systems and cognitive offloading

### Takeaway
There is essentially no controlled evidence that Zettelkasten, PARA, Obsidian, or OneNote improve retention or productivity. The claims are practitioner anecdote. The cognitive-offloading literature shows a trade-off: offloading reliably improves task performance and can free capacity for new learning (saving-enhanced memory), but whatever you offload you tend not to remember yourself. The famous "Google effect" has largely failed to replicate.

### Cited Findings
- Risko & Gilbert (2016), "Cognitive Offloading," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 20(9):676–688. Offloading is defined as physical action that reduces a task's cognitive demands. People offload when they judge it more efficient than using internal memory, and these metacognitive judgments are often inaccurate. — [UCL Discovery](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1508770); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27542527/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/08/160816134021.htm)
- **Saving-enhanced memory** [Lab]: Storm & Stone (2015, *Psychological Science* 26(2):182–188). Saving one file before studying a second improved memory for the second file, because offloading reduced proactive interference. The benefit disappeared when saving was unreliable or the saved material was too small to interfere. A follow-up (Runge et al., 2019) found saving also helped performance on a subsequent unrelated task. — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/saving-old-information-can-boost-memory-for-new-information.html); [UCL follow-up thesis PDF](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10169254/1/An%20investigation%20of%20the%20saving%20enhanced%20memory%20effect.pdf)
- **Google effect** [Replication failure]: Sparrow, Liu & Wegner (2011, *Science* 333:776–778) reported that people remember where information is stored more than the information itself, plus a "Google Stroop" effect. Camerer et al. (2018) did not replicate the Stroop experiment. A later German replication accounting for Sparrow's objections, such as outdated terms like "AltaVista," also failed. Sparrow disputed design differences. Status: not reliably replicated. — [FORRT replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1126/science.1207745/); [PeerJ replication](https://peerj.com/articles/10325); [McGill OSS](https://www.mcgill.ca/oss/article/critical-thinking-technology/digital-amnesia-has-been-exaggerated)
- **PKM / Zettelkasten evidence**: Searches found no controlled studies showing Zettelkasten or "second brain" methods improve learning or retention compared with other note-taking. The only study-like source is a small arXiv case study of researchers' Obsidian knowledge bases, which found that planned retrieval strategies shape how the base is used. Skeptics argue the insight literature concerns incubation and working-memory offloading, not browsing linked notes. Most of the "Zettelkasten principles" are modern reconstructions of Luhmann's practice. — [arXiv 2509.20187](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2509.20187); [brennan.day critique](https://brennan.day/what-have-note-taking-pkms-accomplished-really/); [goryugo](https://goryugo.com/en/zettelkasten)
- The plausible mechanism for the claimed benefit, rewriting ideas into atomic notes in your own words, is generative processing. Practitioner sources assert this but do not test it. — [Zettelkasten search summary sources: affine.pro](https://affine.pro/blog/zettelkasten-method); [brennan.day](https://brennan.day/what-have-note-taking-pkms-accomplished-really/)

### Inferences
- Practical rule: offload what you need to find (reference material such as full FMR text, report specs, client details, project status) and internalize what you need to think with (core concepts, frequently used DAX and SQL patterns, key regulatory rules and thresholds, acronyms used in meetings). Storm & Stone support offloading as a way to clear space, but only when the external store is reliable and trusted.
- A PKM system pays off when notes are written in your own words and paired with retrieval, for example by turning permanent notes into Anki cards. The value of a "second brain" lies in the processing, not the storage. This inference rests on testing-effect and generative-learning evidence, not on PKM-specific studies.
- PARA (Projects/Areas/Resources/Archives) is a filing taxonomy designed for retrieval by actionability. It is not a learning method, and no evidence for or against it was found. It is reasonable for someone running both a day job and a business.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study on PARA, Obsidian, or OneNote outcomes. Claims about them are anecdotal.
- The Storm & Stone search did not surface a large-scale preregistered replication of saving-enhanced memory, so its robustness is uncertain.
- Other cited offloading studies (e.g., Henkel's photo-taking impairment effect, Kelly & Risko on offloading and memory) are not verified in this session.

## 3. Generative AI and learning/retention; using AI as a retrieval partner

### Takeaway
The best causal evidence is two RCTs pointing in opposite directions depending on design. Unrestricted ChatGPT-style help improved practice performance but lowered later unassisted test scores by 17% (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025). A tutor engineered to give one step at a time and withhold answers beat in-class active learning (Kestin et al., Sci Rep 2025). The MIT EEG "cognitive debt" study is a small, non-peer-reviewed preprint under formal methodological critique, and survey studies are correlational. The design rule: AI should make you retrieve and generate, not hand over answers.

### Cited Findings
- **Bastani et al. [RCT]**, "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics," *PNAS* 122(26), 2025 (SSRN preprint July 2024). About 1,000 Turkish high-school students were assigned to "GPT Base" (a ChatGPT-like interface) or "GPT Tutor" (with learning-safeguard prompts). Practice performance rose with AI access. When access was removed, GPT Base students scored 17% lower than controls on exams. GPT Tutor largely mitigated the harm. Mechanism: students used the base model as a crutch, often asking for answers. — [UPenn CHIBE](https://chibe.upenn.edu/publications/generative-ai-without-guardrails-can-harm-learning-evidence-from-high-school-mathematics); [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/unrestricted-generative-ai-harms-high-school-math-learning-by-acting-as-a-crutch/); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)
- **Kestin et al. [RCT, crossover]**, "AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: an RCT," *Scientific Reports* 15:17458 (2025). 194 Harvard intro-physics students. The AI tutor ("PS2 Pal") was prompted to reveal one step at a time and never the full solution. Students learned significantly more in less time (median ~49 vs 60 minutes, per secondary sources) and reported higher engagement. Limits: two topics, one course, short-term learning only, with no long-term retention measured. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/); [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2024/09/professor-tailored-ai-tutor-to-physics-course-engagement-doubled/)
- **Kosmyna et al., "Your Brain on ChatGPT" [Preprint, small n]**, MIT Media Lab, arXiv 2506.08872 (June 2025). Essay writing in three groups: LLM, search engine, and brain-only. 54 participants in sessions 1–3, with only 18 in a session-4 crossover. EEG connectivity was strongest in brain-only, intermediate in search, and weakest in LLM. LLM users reported the lowest ownership of their essays. The authors frame this as accumulating "cognitive debt" over four months. — [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/your-brain-on-chatgpt/); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)
- **Critique**: an arXiv comment (Stankovic et al., Dec 2025/Jan 2026, arXiv 2601.00856) cites limited sample size, reproducibility of analyses, EEG methodology, and inconsistent reporting, and argues for more conservative interpretation. The original is not peer-reviewed as of these searches. — [arXiv 2601.00856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856v1)
- **Gerlich (2025) [Correlational]**, *Societies* 15(1):6. 666 participants. Frequent AI use correlated negatively with critical-thinking scores, mediated by cognitive offloading. Correlations were stronger in ages 17–25, and higher education was protective. The author stresses that causation is not established, and reverse causation is plausible. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/d5c17dd91ce64775a065095d8af730ce); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/ai-tools-may-weaken-critical-thinking-skills-by-encouraging-cognitive-offloading-study-suggests/)
- **Lee et al. (CHI 2025, Microsoft Research/CMU) [Self-report survey]**: 319 knowledge workers, 936 examples of GenAI use at work. Higher confidence in GenAI was associated with less critical thinking, and higher self-confidence with more. Critical effort shifted toward verifying, integrating, and stewarding AI output. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=1135061); [author PDF](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)

### Inferences
- The common thread across Bastani, Kestin, and the offloading literature is that learning tracks how much the learner generates and retrieves. AI that supplies answers substitutes for that work. AI that withholds answers and gives hints can amplify it.
- Practical patterns for using AI as a retrieval partner, inferred from these RCTs and the testing-effect literature:
  1. Attempt first: write the DAX measure, SQL query, or FMR interpretation yourself, then ask AI to critique it rather than produce it.
  2. Quiz mode: paste your own notes or an FMR chapter and instruct "Ask me one question at a time; do not reveal answers; give hints only after I attempt; at the end list what I missed." This replicates Kestin's one-step-at-a-time design.
  3. Have AI draft candidate flashcards from source text, then edit them yourself. Matuschak notes good prompts are hard to write, so AI drafts plus human editing are likely better than either alone, but this is untested.
  4. Explain-back: teach a concept to the AI and ask it to probe gaps (see section 4).
  5. Keep "no-AI reps": periodically solve problems unassisted, because Bastani shows performance with AI overstates learning.
- For audit-sensitive DoD financial work, verifying AI output against primary sources (FMR volume/chapter, OMB circulars) is a professional obligation as well as a learning tactic. Lee et al. found critical effort shifts to verification, so build that step in explicitly.

### Gaps
- No RCT measures long-term (months) retention effects of AI-tutor use in adult professionals.
- The MIT preprint's behavioral finding that LLM users had trouble quoting their own essays is widely reported, but I could not verify the exact percentage because arxiv.org was blocked.
- Other 2025–2026 studies (e.g., any peer-reviewed version of the MIT study, or newer AI-tutor RCTs from late 2026) were not found in these searches.

## 4. Applying this to technical professional learning (SQL/DAX/Excel, DoD FMR, acronyms; teaching, worked examples, deliberate practice)

### Takeaway
The evidence base (retrieval practice g ≈ 0.5–0.7; learning-by-teaching meta-analyses; Deng's finding that practice questions beat cards per item) supports a mixed system. Use spaced flashcards for discrete facts such as acronyms, FMR thresholds, and function syntax. Use worked-example study followed by unassisted problem-solving for SQL, DAX, and Excel. Explain or teach material to consolidate it. Use a reliable searchable external store for everything else.

### Cited Findings
- Retrieval practice produces medium-to-large learning gains in both lab and classroom settings (Rowland g ≈ 0.50; Adesope classroom g ≈ 0.67). — [Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2019.00005/full); [WSU PDF](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)
- Practice questions were associated with more score gain per item than flashcards (445 questions ≈ 1,700 cards ≈ +1 Step 1 point). — [Deng et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26498443/)
- Learning by teaching ("protégé effect"): meta-analyses (Kobayashi 2019; Lachner et al. 2021; Ribosa & Duran) support learning gains from teaching. Studying with the expectation of teaching beats studying for a test (Fiorella & Mayer 2013, 2014). Teaching a non-interactive audience, such as recording an explanation, also helps in several studies (Hoogerheide et al. 2014), though some studies found null or negative effects. — [Educ Psych Review 2021 (Utrecht)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09643-4); [PMC9979973 (critique of Ribosa & Duran)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9979973/)
- Effective AI tutoring for problem domains withholds full solutions and scaffolds step by step. — [Kestin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)
- Cloze deletions efficiently convert prose (such as regulation text) into reviewable items, but "understand before you memorize" comes first. — [SuperMemo 20 rules](https://www.supermemo.com/en/archives1990-2015/articles/20rules)

### Inferences (practical playbook — inferred from cited evidence, not directly tested in professional settings)
- **Acronyms and domain vocabulary** (e.g., the PPBE, CFO Act, USSGL, FIAR, and ADA clusters): this is ideal Anki material. Make cards both directions (acronym→meaning, and meaning plus context→acronym) with one fact per card, plus a usage sentence as a cloze. Under the minimum information principle, never put a list of 10 acronyms on one card.
- **DoD FMR / regulations**: don't memorize text. Memorize the map (which volume and chapter governs what), key decision rules and thresholds, and frequently cited definitions. Keep the authoritative text in the external store, which follows the offload-to-find rule. Make cards that ask "Where would you look to determine X?" and "What is the rule when Y?" Rewrite rules in your own words before carding them. Re-verify cards when FMR chapters are updated.
- **SQL / DAX / Excel**: study a worked example, then faded examples, then solve a fresh problem unassisted, and card the patterns ("Write the DAX to compute YTD with a fiscal year ending 30 Sep," "Which function returns ... ?"). Problem-style cards and small weekly practice sets mirror Deng's finding that questions beat definition cards. Use AI only after an attempt, as critic or hint-giver.
- **Teaching / protégé effect**: write short internal how-to notes, brown-bag explainers, or client-facing explainers for the business. These both consolidate learning and produce reusable assets. Recording a 5-minute explanation is a low-cost version.
- **Deliberate practice**: target known weak spots, use Anki's lapse statistics as a weakness detector, get fast feedback (query results, AI critique, a colleague's review), and keep sessions short and daily. 10–20 minutes of reviews fits a dual-career schedule.
- **System design for someone working a day job and building a business**: (1) one trusted capture and reference store (OneNote/Obsidian/SharePoint), organized for retrieval, e.g., PARA; (2) one SRS deck per domain, using FSRS at 0.85–0.90 retention; (3) AI used in quiz or critique mode, not answer mode; (4) a weekly teach-back or write-up.

### Gaps
- Worked-example effect and expertise-reversal effect (Sweller; Kalyuga): these are well established in the cognitive-load literature but were not searched this session, so they need citation by the report writer from another researcher's notes or a primary source.
- Deliberate-practice evidence (Ericsson; Macnamara et al. 2014 meta-analysis finding that deliberate practice explains a modest share of performance variance, smaller in professions) was not verified this session.
- I found no studies specifically on learning SQL/DAX via spaced repetition or on learning government financial regulations. Recommendations are extrapolated from general learning science.
- Kobayashi (2019) effect sizes for learning by teaching were not retrieved.
